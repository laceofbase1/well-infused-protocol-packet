Functional Fusion Protocol

How the protocol works

Four documents, one protocol. The provider works from the formulary and the quick guide; the patient leaves with the pad and the wheel. They are built as one system, in one visual language, so a patient who looks at the sheet in their hand and a provider who looks at the reference on the desk are looking at the same thing.

Why it is built this way

The point of building it this way is that care gets added when the labs call for it, and a patient can see why. The wheel does the work the formulary cannot: it shows that the foundation alone moves someone in the right direction, and that several therapies turning together move them faster. That is a clinical argument made visible, and it is what makes the conversation about the whole protocol rather than one item at a time.

The Formulary

Everything the clinic offers, and what each thing is for.

Internal · attached to the email Version 2.5

What it is

Four sheets covering the whole menu of care: the foundation, hormones and peptides, infusions and procedures, and memberships. It is the provider's reference for what exists and what it treats, grouped the way a protocol is actually built.

How it is used

Open it at the lab review. It answers “what do we have, and what is it for” so the provider can build a protocol from findings rather than memory. It is not a patient handout and not a list anybody chooses from: it carries no prices for that reason, and those live on the quick guide instead.

When it changes

It changes as products are added. Send anything new and it goes in, and a new version comes back.

Printing

Print at the office. It will keep changing, so there is no point having it printed properly.

The Prescription Pad

One sheet. The provider fills it in during the visit.

Goes to the patientFinal, 19 October 2026

What it is

The clinical order, and the thing the patient leaves holding. Every pathway the clinic offers is on it. The provider marks what the labs call for, writes the detail on the line beside it, and sets the return date.

How it is used

Filled in with the patient, in front of them, during the visit. What is marked is prescribed; what is blank is not, and the sheet says so in print, so nothing is ambiguous later. The foundation is printed as already marked, because it is not chosen.

When it changes

Settled. This is the final design and it should not need to change.

Printing

Send this one to a printer, on good stock. The design is settled, so it can go now. It is handled at every visit and the patient leaves holding it, so it should not feel like something run off at the desk.

Your Protocol

How the protocol works, explained in one picture.

Goes to the patientThe wheel

What it is

Six wheels, each a therapy, every one of them meshed with the patient at the center. Any single wheel turns them. More of them turning drives them harder.

How it is used

Handed to the patient and talked through, alongside the pad. It is the answer to “why are you recommending more than one thing?”: not because more is better to sell, but because the labs indicate it and several therapies working together move someone faster than the foundation can alone.

When it changes

Draft for Shawn and Staci to react to. Nothing is printed until it is agreed.

Printing

Printer and good stock, same as the pad, once the two of you agree it. It goes into a patient's hands and gets talked through, so it should feel like a piece of the practice rather than a handout.

Adding to it, and printing it

The formulary will change often, so it gets printed at the office. It is built from a list, so anything new goes in and a fresh version comes back: send the product and what it is for. The quick guide is the same whenever a price moves. Neither is worth sending to a printer, because both will be out of date by the time they arrive.

The prescription pad and Your Protocol are the two that get properly printed, on good stock, by a printer. The pad is settled and can go now. The wheel goes as soon as the two of you agree it. Those are the pieces a patient handles, so they are the ones worth the paper.
